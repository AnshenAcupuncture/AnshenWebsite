Those fire cupping circles: what they are, and what they mean

If you've seen the round marks cupping can leave and wondered whether they're bruises, or whether they hurt, here's what's actually going on.

Fire cupping has a very recognisable signature: soft, round marks where the cups have sat. They tend to raise a lot of questions, and understandably so. Let's walk through what they are, why they appear, and what they aren't.

First, what fire cupping actually is

Fire cupping is a traditional Chinese medicine technique. A flame is used to warm the air inside a glass cup, and the cup is then placed on the skin. As the air cools it contracts, creating a gentle vacuum that draws the skin and the tissue just beneath it upward into the cup. To be clear, the flame never touches you — it only heats the inside of the cup a moment before it's applied.

The cups may be left resting in one place, or moved slowly across an oiled area. It's a surprisingly relaxing sensation for most people: a steady drawing or tugging rather than anything sharp.

So why do the marks appear?

The circles aren't bruises in the ordinary sense. A bruise comes from an impact. Cupping marks come from gentle suction drawing circulation toward the surface of the skin within that ring. In the language of Chinese medicine, the cups are said to bring stagnation to the surface so it can move and clear.

The traditional view reads darker marks as areas of greater stagnation. In practical terms, colour depends more on local circulation, skin type and suction strength than any single cause.

Because of that, the marks vary a lot from person to person, and even from session to session on the same person. Some areas barely change colour; others come up more deeply. Over a course of treatment, many people notice the marks becoming lighter as things ease.

Do they hurt, and how long do they last?

The marks themselves aren't sore to look at or to touch, though the area can feel a little tender for a day, much like after a firm massage. They typically fade over several days, sometimes up to a week or so, and then disappear completely.

If you've got something coming up (a wedding, a beach day, a photo shoot), just mention it. Cupping can be placed where clothing will cover it, kept lighter, or saved for another time.

One thing worth flagging first

Before any cupping, it helps to mention a few things: any skin conditions, whether you bruise very easily, any bleeding disorders or blood-thinning medication, and whether you're pregnant. None of these are automatic reasons to avoid it, but they help us decide together whether cupping is right for you on the day.
